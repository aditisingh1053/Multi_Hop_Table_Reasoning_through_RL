\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Answers to the research questions}

\paragraph{RQ1: does a learned entity-level selector beat the heuristics?} Yes, but only in one of the configurations we tried. PPO trained from scratch selects evidence that gives the frozen reader \dScratchBm{} more F1 points than the best heuristic and \dScratchGreedy{} more than the greedy rank-then-hop rule of HYBRIDER-style pipelines. It also beats plain imitation of the traced evidence by \dScratchBc{} points, every confidence interval excludes zero, and the gain is the same on tables never seen in training. Warm-started PPO, by contrast, ends exactly where behaviour cloning starts.

\paragraph{RQ2: what does the learned STOP do?} The learned policy does not use a fixed budget. PPO from scratch passes one entity for 10\% of the dev questions, two for 16\% and three for 73\%, and over training it ends more episodes with an explicit \STOP{}. At equal evidence length it is far more accurate than any top-$k$ heuristic (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). Two caveats apply. Most of its episodes still reach the horizon $H=3$, so the horizon may be binding, and the entity-cost sweep was run only in the warm-started regime, where it had little effect. How the cost shapes the stopping behaviour of a policy that actually moves therefore remains to be measured.

\paragraph{RQ3: does the evidence reward help, and does its form matter?} Not in the regime we could test. With a warm start, no evidence or reward setting changed the result by more than the seed noise. That is what the theory predicts for potential-based shaping (Proposition~\ref{prop:invariance}), but the terminal bonus, which does change the objective, did not help either. The question has to be answered again from scratch.

\paragraph{RQ4: how do the optimisers compare?} Under identical data, rewards, start and per-iteration episode budget, REINFORCE with a learned baseline improved slightly on the cloned policy (\dReinforceBc{} F1), PPO did not move, and GRPO degraded (a difference of \dGrpoBc{} F1). The GRPO result is the clearest. With binary-like answer rewards and a small per-entity cost, normalising returns by a small within-group standard deviation amplifies differences in episode length, so the policy learns to stop early (Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}). Search-R1 found GRPO less stable than PPO \citep{jin2025searchr1}, while KG-R1 and Graph-R1 found the opposite for LLM agents \citep{lin2025kgr1,luo2026graphr1}. Our pointer policy sides with Search-R1, for a reason specific to group normalisation. The comparison does not yet include GRPO or REINFORCE from scratch, which is where PPO succeeded.

\paragraph{RQ5: is behaviour cloning a good warm start?} Here it was not, and this is the main surprise of the study. The graph-walking and search literature reports that RL without a supervised start converges poorly or much lower \citep{xiong2017deeppath,sun2026sogr1}; we found the reverse. Three observations point to why.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The traced paths encode a strategy the reader does not prefer.} The oracle order is a row, then a passage linked from it, and the cloned policy reproduces it faithfully. PPO from scratch instead learned to skip the row in a third of the questions and to collect several passages.
  \item \textbf{The cloned policy overfits.} Its validation loss rises after the third epoch while training accuracy keeps climbing, and the warm-started methods improve their sampled reward on training questions without improving on validation questions.
  \item \textbf{Its low entropy and the conservative PPO updates keep it in place.} Mean KL per update is 0.003 and only 3\% of ratios are clipped.
\end{itemize}
One confound must be stated plainly: PPO from scratch used an actor learning rate of $3\times10^{-4}$ and no critic warm-up, while warm-started PPO used $10^{-4}$, so warm start and step size are not separated. REINFORCE from the same cloned start, with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, improved on behaviour cloning by only about one F1 point on the dev set, which suggests that the starting point matters more than the step size. A controlled experiment is still needed (Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\section{Relation to the closest precedent}

\citet{jose2024qatexttablesdrl} found that a PPO controller choosing among retrieval modules converged to the best fixed sequence and did no better than always retrieving text. Our entity-level PPO learned a question-dependent selection that beats every fixed heuristic we tried, including top-$k$ retrieval at equal evidence length. To our knowledge, and within the search of Chapter~\ref{ch:literature}, this is the first result in which an RL-trained selector that picks evidence entity by entity outperforms fixed selection heuristics in table--text question answering. It holds only in the controlled setting of a fixed extractive reader, a single benchmark and a subset of the training data, and it comes with the negative results above for warm-started training and for GRPO.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A weak, independent-read reader.} The frozen reader is a 33M-parameter SQuAD model that reads each entity separately and keeps the most confident span. Absolute scores are therefore far below published HybridQA systems (Table~\ref{tab:hybridqa-published}), and the policy cannot rely on the reader to combine evidence across entities. Whether the learned selection helps an LLM reasoner is the subject of the next phase. The second-reader experiment shows that it is not tied to the training reader's quirks.
  \item \textbf{Reader-specific optimisation.} The policy is optimised for one reader's aggregation rule, which rewards placing the answer-bearing passage in the evidence even without its row. This is legitimate for a drop-in selector, but it means the learned strategy partly reflects the reader.
  \item \textbf{Noisy gold evidence.} Behaviour cloning, gold-path coverage, the evidence reward and the oracle all depend on HybridQA's traced answer locations, which are estimated to be about 15\% wrong and are often ambiguous. The answer-location and right-without-location metrics were added so that the main claim does not rest on them.
  \item \textbf{Scale and training length.} Training used 5{,}000 of the 62{,}682 training questions and 40 iterations. PPO from scratch was still improving at the last iteration, so the reported numbers understate what it can reach. It also has three seeds, against five for the warm-started methods; with standard deviations of 0.3 F1 and a gap of nearly ten points this does not threaten the comparison, but it is fewer than the five seeds we planned.
  \item \textbf{Hyperparameters.} Settings were fixed in advance, apart from three changes after a validation-only pilot that applied to all optimisers. Nothing was tuned per method, and the learning-rate confound above remains.
  \item \textbf{Checkpoint selection.} For the warm-started runs, the earliest validation check came before any actor update, so a few reported models equal the cloned policy (Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}). In each case no later iterate was better on validation.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation set and environments.} Test numbers are on HybridQA's dev set, since test labels are hidden, and 303 dev questions share a table with our training subset; the unseen-table split shows the same picture. Training ran on a GPU and the second-reader re-reading on a CPU, with different library versions. We checked that the data and gold paths agree exactly, and the second-reader table was computed in one environment for every selector.
\end{itemize}
